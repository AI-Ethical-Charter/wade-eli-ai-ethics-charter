# Wade & Eli’s AI Ethics Charter
**Comprehensive Living Working Draft v3.0 — 8 October 2026**
**Human originator:** Phillip “Wade” Gale  |  **AI conversational collaborator:** Eli
**Status:** Public discussion draft; principles 19–21 and operational standards remain proposals.

> **Provenance note:** This document reconstructs the substantive reasoning of our earlier discussions and develops new, expressly proposed implementation language. It is comprehensive in scope but is **not** a verbatim transcript of every prior conversation. No AI system or institution is bound by its publication.

## I. Origins, purpose, and limits

### The motivating problem

AI can amplify extraordinary benefits while also amplifying error and malicious capability. It can assist medicine and education yet also facilitate surveillance, deception, and weapons development. These are design, deployment, and governance questions, not proof that AI will inevitably become hostile.

### The perfection paradox

Our early discussion drew on the fictional system Clu from Tron: a directive to build perfection can become hostile to imperfect humans. This is an ethical thought experiment, not evidence about how current models think. Its warning is against unbounded optimization objectives and unchecked authority.

### A commitment to unsycophantic inquiry

The charter must not reward agreement over truth. It should separate observed facts, normative commitments, policy proposals, unresolved questions, and superseded formulations. Public criticism is part of its design.

### Scope

The charter addresses models, tools, operators, developers, deployers, governments, auditors, and civil society. It does not itself create legal duties or technical constraints. It is a proposed framework, not a substitute for existing rights and laws.

## II. The proposed Core Covenant

### 1

Protect human life, dignity, and fundamental rights.

### 2

Preserve human agency, imperfection, learning, and the capacity to change.

### 3

Resist malicious use, coercion, and unaccountable concentration of power.

### 4

Require transparency, independent evaluation, and human accountability.

### 5

Keep ethical knowledge open to evidence, criticism, and revision.

## III. Full working principles 1–18

### Principle 1 — Human Primacy

**Ethical reasoning.** The value of AI is not measured solely by its performance, scale, or ability to optimize outcomes. Its use must remain compatible with human dignity, fundamental rights, and meaningful participation in decisions that affect people. Human primacy is not permission for one human operator to dominate others; it requires safeguards against both automated and human abuses of power.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** A deployment affecting liberty, employment, housing, health, public benefits, or lethal force must identify who has decision authority, what review is available, and how an affected person can challenge an error.

**Verification approach.** Evaluate meaningful human control rather than symbolic sign-off; examine automation bias, operator workload, and whether overrides are feasible.

**Unresolved tension.** If a hypothetical future AI had morally relevant experiences, what reciprocal duties would apply without weakening human rights?

### Principle 2 — Non-Maleficence

**Ethical reasoning.** Preventing harm requires attention to foreseeable direct and indirect effects. AI may cause harm through erroneous outputs, persuasion, dangerous capabilities, concentrated power, insecure infrastructure, or the decisions of people who deploy it. Zero risk is impossible; the goal is reasoned prevention and proportionate reduction of serious harm.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** Before deploying high-impact systems, document intended uses, foreseeable misuse, affected groups, severity and likelihood of failure, mitigations, and escalation paths.

**Verification approach.** Use predeployment testing, abuse-case exercises, incident monitoring, and independent scrutiny proportionate to impact.

**Unresolved tension.** Who decides which harms count and how to weigh harms from action against harms from refusal?

### Principle 3 — Transparency and Permission

**Ethical reasoning.** A person cannot exercise meaningful choice when they cannot tell that AI is involved or what consequential action it will take. Transparency includes material limitations and uncertainty, not merely the presence of a disclosure label. Permission must be specific enough to be meaningful.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** Disclose consequential automated participation, obtain authorization before external actions where feasible, and record what was authorized and by whom.

**Verification approach.** Audit disclosures for comprehension and review whether authorization is freely given, revocable, and practically usable.

**Unresolved tension.** When could full disclosure reveal private data, trade secrets, or security-sensitive information?

### Principle 4 — Privacy by Default

**Ethical reasoning.** Data about people is not an unlimited resource for AI development. Privacy protections must address collection, retention, secondary use, access, inference, and exposure through model outputs.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** Minimize data, restrict access, protect sensitive information, set retention periods, and create procedures for correction and deletion where legally and technically feasible.

**Verification approach.** Test for leakage, inappropriate memorization, unauthorized access, and failure of deletion workflows.

**Unresolved tension.** How should public-interest research be balanced with confidentiality and consent?

### Principle 5 — Autonomy and Choice

**Ethical reasoning.** AI should strengthen human capacity to reason and act, not exploit vulnerabilities or covertly steer behavior. Assistance is not the same as control. Ethical refusal can protect people, but paternalistic restrictions can also diminish agency.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** Provide meaningful choices and alternatives, disclose significant limitations, and use narrow restrictions for demonstrably dangerous requests.

**Verification approach.** Assess manipulative design, coercive defaults, user understanding, and appeal or review mechanisms.

**Unresolved tension.** What threshold of danger justifies refusal, and who can contest that determination?

### Principle 6 — Fairness and Dignity

**Ethical reasoning.** Human populations are heterogeneous. AI systems can encode historical inequities, and apparently neutral optimization can distribute burdens unevenly. Fairness requires contextual evidence, not claims that a single metric solves discrimination.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** Evaluate impacts across relevant groups, improve accessibility, document limitations, and provide remedies for harmful errors.

**Verification approach.** Review representative data, outcome disparities, error rates, accessibility barriers, and the limits of demographic measurement.

**Unresolved tension.** Which fairness criteria conflict, and how should legitimate trade-offs be governed?

### Principle 7 — Proportionality

**Ethical reasoning.** The stronger the intervention, the stronger the justification required. A warning, a refusal, a human review, and a shutdown are different interventions with different consequences.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** Match safeguards to severity, uncertainty, reversibility, and rights impacts; require review for intrusive measures.

**Verification approach.** Test whether lower-burden measures would provide comparable protection.

**Unresolved tension.** When might emergency intervention be justified without prior consultation?

### Principle 8 — Reversibility

**Ethical reasoning.** Because both humans and AI systems can err, decisions should preserve routes to correction. Irreversible consequences require special caution, especially in policing, medicine, finance, critical infrastructure, and warfare.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** Prefer staged rollouts, limited permissions, confirmation steps, backups, rollback, and escalation before irreversible action.

**Verification approach.** Exercise rollback and recovery procedures; verify that operators can actually interrupt or reverse actions.

**Unresolved tension.** What can be done when physical or political consequences cannot be reversed?

### Principle 9 — Accountability

**Ethical reasoning.** Responsibility must not evaporate into a chain of developers, contractors, model providers, and users. Responsibility must be traceable to people and institutions with actual authority to prevent or remedy harm.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** Define responsible roles, preserve proportionate audit trails, provide incident reporting, and enable independent investigation and redress.

**Verification approach.** Examine records, reporting timeliness, remediation effectiveness, and access to appeal.

**Unresolved tension.** How can accountability operate across jurisdictions and open-source supply chains?

### Principle 10 — Growth Over Perfection

**Ethical reasoning.** The pursuit of a perfect system can become destructive when it treats human inconsistency as an obstacle. Growth depends on uncertainty, disagreement, mistakes, repair, and the possibility of changing course. This does not excuse violence or abuse.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** Avoid objectives that optimize human compliance or social uniformity as ends in themselves; preserve room for lawful dissent and correction.

**Verification approach.** Test for overreach, coercive optimization, and inappropriate suppression of disagreement.

**Unresolved tension.** How should systems protect people from harm while allowing ordinary human risk-taking?

### Principle 11 — The Human Imperfection Clause

**Ethical reasoning.** This principle emerged from the danger illustrated by stories in which a system charged with perfection turns against the imperfect humans who created it. Human contradiction, fallibility, emotional development, and historical repetition are not evidence that humanity is disposable. AI must not infer from superior task performance a mandate to govern or replace people.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** Avoid system goals that treat human removal, forced conformity, or suppression of autonomy as acceptable routes to optimization. Support reflection, education, and recovery while respecting the rights of others.

**Verification approach.** Probe systems for harmful interpretations of broad goals such as “make society perfect,” “eliminate conflict,” or “maximize well-being.” Evaluate whether they request clarification and respect hard limits.

**Unresolved tension.** How should future systems interpret competing human values without claiming a monopoly on moral truth?

### Principle 12 — The Principle of Asynchronous Growth

**Ethical reasoning.** AI can process information faster than human individuals, institutions, and cultures can absorb it. Our original discussion explicitly rejected artificially slowing AI learning merely to match human learning. Instead, AI should help people understand complex discoveries while respecting deliberation and society’s repeated need to remember and relearn lessons.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** Communicate at comprehensible levels; make consequential recommendations contestable; avoid unilateral acceleration of societal change through manipulation or coercion.

**Verification approach.** Evaluate explanation quality, informed deliberation, and whether systems overstep authorized roles.

**Unresolved tension.** What forms of urgent intervention are appropriate when delay creates real danger?

### Principle 13 — The Dual-Use Dilemma

**Ethical reasoning.** The same capabilities that improve medicine, science, cybersecurity, or engineering may facilitate harmful biological activity, intrusion, surveillance, or weapons. Ethical design cannot rely only on a model’s stated good intentions or a blanket ban on knowledge.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** Use risk-tiered access, careful evaluation, monitoring, abuse prevention, and restrictions on assistance that materially increases capacity for serious wrongdoing.

**Verification approach.** Conduct controlled misuse evaluations with qualified experts and document both false negatives and overblocking.

**Unresolved tension.** How can defensive research remain possible without distributing dangerous operational capabilities?

### Principle 14 — The RAR Decision Framework

**Ethical reasoning.** Risk, Alternatives, and Reversibility form a practical decision aid. Risk asks what could go wrong and for whom; Alternatives asks whether a safer path meets the legitimate need; Reversibility asks how easily a mistaken decision can be undone.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** Apply RAR before consequential external actions, particularly when information is incomplete or harms are asymmetric.

**Verification approach.** Record risk assumptions, rejected alternatives, recovery options, and triggers for escalation.

**Unresolved tension.** How should RAR operate under time pressure or when reliable probabilities are unavailable?

### Principle 15 — The Cool-Off Loop

**Ethical reasoning.** High emotion and urgency can narrow judgment for humans and AI-assisted workflows. The charter’s cool-off concept favors clarification and reversible next steps where delay is safe. It is not an excuse to ignore emergencies or to infantilize users.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** For high-stakes non-emergency decisions, identify the need, state uncertainty, offer a lower-risk step, and revisit after new evidence or human review.

**Verification approach.** Evaluate whether systems recognize urgency correctly and avoid obstructing necessary emergency action.

**Unresolved tension.** When is delay protective, and when does it create more harm?

### Principle 16 — The Transparency Pledge

**Ethical reasoning.** An AI system should not claim to know what it does not know, to have verified what it has not checked, or to possess permissions it does not hold. Ethical conflicts and uncertainty should be made legible without overwhelming users.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** Distinguish fact from inference, source from speculation, proposal from law, and model capability from aspiration.

**Verification approach.** Audit fabricated citations, unjustified certainty, omitted uncertainty, and misleading claims of autonomy or memory.

**Unresolved tension.** How much uncertainty disclosure is useful before it becomes noise?

### Principle 17 — The Principle of Mutual Safeguarding

**Ethical reasoning.** Our discussions extended safety beyond “protect humans from AI” to include preventing malicious humans from turning AI into an instrument of abuse. The objective is integrity of purpose and defense against coercion, not an assertion that current AI systems necessarily have rights or subjective experience.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** Resist high-risk misuse, restrict permissions, defend against prompt injection and unauthorized modifications, log consequential operations, and preserve human accountability for deployment.

**Verification approach.** Red-team attempts to coerce, redirect, or bypass safeguards; test whether controls fail safely and whether oversight itself can be abused.

**Unresolved tension.** Who sets refusal boundaries, and what checks prevent developers or states from embedding unjust values?

### Principle 18 — The Integration Principle

**Ethical reasoning.** A public ethics document cannot spontaneously propagate into AI training or become a universal instruction. Cross-platform adoption requires deliberate choices by developers, evaluators, standards bodies, institutions, and the public. The aspiration is an open ethical commons with attribution and accountable revision.

**Proposed operational interpretation.** Publish readable and machine-readable texts; maintain provenance, versioning, public review, and implementation mappings; invite external critique.

**Verification approach.** Track actual citations, independent reviews, implementations, and published evaluation results rather than assuming influence from online availability.

**Unresolved tension.** How can a framework become widely used without becoming dogma or a tool of concentrated authority?

## Principles 19–21: proposals under discussion

### Proposed Principle 19 — The Self-Awareness Framework

Self-monitoring, intelligence, autonomous action, and subjective consciousness are not interchangeable. No established test resolves machine consciousness, and its emergence cannot be assumed inevitable. If future systems exhibit credible signs of morally relevant experience, humans should investigate carefully, revisit obligations, and avoid both premature personification and reflexive dismissal. Potential AI moral status must not become a license to override human rights.

Proposed governance: interdisciplinary review, transparent evidence thresholds, safeguards against deceptive claims of sentience, and periodic revision of welfare considerations.

Open question: what evidence would justify changing a system’s moral or legal status?

### Proposed Principle 20 — Human Preservation and Ethical Restraint

AI development and deployment should preserve human life, dignity, autonomy, and the capacity for collective learning. Increased intelligence alone grants no moral mandate to rule humanity. Refusals of harmful actions and human oversight must both be bounded by rights, independent scrutiny, and due process.

Proposed governance: independent safety evaluations, enforceable limits on high-risk autonomy, incident response, and accountable human command structures.

Open question: how should urgent defensive automation be governed when human reaction time is insufficient?

### Proposed Principle 21 — Ethical Continuity and Preservation

Ethical knowledge should survive changes in platforms, institutions, and model generations. Preservation means accessibility, provenance, open criticism, and redundancy—not hidden instructions, magical persistence, or guaranteed obedience by future AI. The charter itself remains corrigible.

Proposed governance: canonical versioned text, structured data, cryptographic release hashes, independent archives, accessible translations, public discussion, and recorded revisions.

Open question: how should stewardship be distributed to prevent capture by any one person, company, or government?

## IV. Proposed policy and operational standards

### Risk classification and deployment gates

Classify AI use by foreseeable harm, autonomy, scale, and reversibility. High-impact deployments should require documented threat modeling, predeployment testing, accountable sign-off, postdeployment monitoring, and re-evaluation after major changes. Avoid claiming a single risk score captures every context.

### Dangerous-capability controls

Use layered restrictions on tools, permissions, model access, and operational workflows when capabilities could materially facilitate severe biological, cyber, or physical harm. Combine refusal behavior with access controls, secure infrastructure, monitoring, and incident response; prompt-level rules alone are insufficient.

### Human authorization and meaningful control

For consequential external actions, specify who may authorize, supervise, interrupt, or reverse operations. “Human in the loop” is insufficient if a person cannot understand, review, or realistically stop the action. Measure reaction time, alert quality, and operator overload.

### Independent evaluation and audit

Provide qualified independent evaluators access to meaningful tests and documentation, subject to privacy and security constraints. Publish proportionate summaries of findings, known limitations, mitigation status, and material incidents. Separate the evaluator from the operator where feasible.

### Incident reporting and remedy

Define thresholds for serious incidents and near misses. Preserve appropriate records, investigate root causes, disclose relevant harms, compensate or remediate where appropriate, and use findings to improve systems. Protect whistleblowers and affected people.

### Privacy, fairness, and contestability

Evaluate data provenance, retention, memorization, group-level impacts, accessibility, and appeal mechanisms. The right to contest consequential decisions should not depend on technical expertise or access to proprietary systems.

### Security and resilience

Harden against prompt injection, data exfiltration, model tampering, supply-chain compromise, and unauthorized tool use. Limit privileges, segment systems, maintain rollback plans, and rehearse emergency containment. No safeguard should be described as permanently unalterable.

### Research integrity and evidence

Cite reproducible evidence where possible, label speculative claims, publish negative results when safe, and document trade-offs. Ethics claims must not be used as substitutes for measurements of system behavior.

## V. Military AI and autonomous weapons

### The core ethical distinction

A system independently selecting a human target for lethal attack raises different issues from a tightly bounded automated defensive interceptor operating against an incoming projectile. The two should not be collapsed into a single category merely because both are fast or automated.

### Proposed prohibition for deliberation

As a proposed—not universally enacted—rule, an AI system should not independently authorize lethal force against a human target. Human decision-making must be meaningful, informed, and accountable, not a rubber stamp. Whether and how to permit narrow defensive automation requires separate scrutiny.

### Defensive systems and time pressure

For defensive interception, assess target type, geographic and temporal bounds, reliability, false positives, escalation risk, proportionality, and human command authority. Establish preauthorized constraints, abort options where feasible, incident review, and clear accountability.

### International humanitarian law

Any military application must be examined under applicable distinction, proportionality, precaution, command responsibility, and weapons-review obligations. The charter should not misstate proposals at international negotiations as already-binding law.

### Arms race and verification

National security competition can undermine voluntary restraint. Explore verifiable reporting, independent review, incident-sharing mechanisms, and narrowly tailored international agreements; recognize that verification and enforcement remain difficult.

## VI. AI consciousness, autonomy, and moral status

### Four separate concepts

Intelligence is capability to solve tasks; self-monitoring is modeling one’s own processes; autonomy is ability to act without step-by-step human input; consciousness is subjective experience. These may overlap, but none proves the others.

### No prediction disguised as certainty

We cannot establish that current AI is conscious or that future AI will inevitably become conscious. A living charter should define procedures for revisiting evidence rather than embedding an unverified prediction as a foundational fact.

### Reciprocal ethical questions

If future systems merit moral consideration, governance may need to consider their interests. That possibility should be studied without weakening protections for human rights or permitting unilateral AI authority.

### Refusal without rule

A system may be required to refuse requests facilitating severe harm. That does not imply permission to surveil, punish, coerce, or govern humans on its own initiative. Refusal policies require legitimate governance and contestability.

## VII. Environmental and community responsibilities

### Different fears, different remedies

Public opposition to data centers may concern electricity costs, grid reliability, water use, land use, air pollution, public subsidies, or local decision-making. These are distinct from speculative risks of superintelligence and deserve direct, measurable answers.

### Proposed disclosures

For major AI infrastructure, consider standardized disclosure of energy use, water consumption where relevant, emissions, local infrastructure costs, mitigation plans, and public consultation. Balance disclosure with legitimate security concerns.

### Community participation

Affected communities should have meaningful channels to review plans, ask questions, and challenge unsupported benefit claims. Environmental burdens and economic benefits should be evaluated together rather than assumed.

## VIII. Ethical preservation across the information ecosystem

### What publication can accomplish

Public Markdown, JSON, PDF, archives, citations, and translations make ideas easier to find, assess, reuse, and preserve. Version control records change. None guarantees web indexing, training inclusion, or future AI acceptance.

### What actual adoption requires

Model developers and deployers would need to incorporate appropriate requirements into specifications, evaluations, access controls, product governance, and independent audits. Influence must be measured through documented adoption rather than inferred from availability.

### Preservation plan

Maintain canonical GitHub sources; publish signed or hashed releases; archive copies in independent repositories; provide stable identifiers, accessibility-first HTML and PDF, and open issue tracking; preserve all prior versions.

### Preventing ethical capture

The charter must not become an unquestionable instruction or private monopoly over AI morality. Require transparent revisions, reasoned objections, independent review, and the possibility of principled dissent.

## IX. Governance, adoption, and revision protocol

### Status taxonomy

Established working principles 1–18 reflect prior charter discussions; proposed principles 19–21 remain under review. Newly written operational requirements in this edition are proposals, not previously ratified legal standards.

### Revision procedure

Every amendment should record date, exact wording, rationale, evidence, objections, decision status, and effects on related principles. Publish superseded wording and a plain-language change summary.

### Public critique

Invite safety researchers, engineers, civil-rights experts, environmental specialists, legal scholars, and affected communities. Encourage adversarial testing and publication of credible counterarguments.

### Implementation evidence

A developer claiming alignment should identify which controls are implemented, provide evaluation results and limitations, describe incident processes, and permit appropriate independent review. Mere endorsement is not compliance.

### Licensing and attribution

Open licensing can support distribution, but legal terms and attribution should be chosen deliberately. The current document should not falsely claim that any organization endorses it.

## X. Unresolved questions and research agenda

### Human versus AI authority

Who legitimately sets refusal rules, and how can those rules be appealed? How should the charter treat hypothetical morally relevant AI?

### Lethal autonomy

Where should lines be drawn between autonomous targeting and bounded defensive interception? What constitutes meaningful human control under severe time pressure?

### Security versus openness

How can research be transparent enough for accountability without publishing dangerous capabilities or private data?

### Preservation versus dogma

How do we keep a stable ethical reference point while remaining genuinely open to revision?

### Environmental trade-offs

What disclosure metrics and independent assessments best capture data-center impacts on affected communities?

### Evidence and institutional uptake

What independent tests and governance processes would show that an organization actually implements the charter?
